1 Introduction 1.1 Background and motivation Endoscopic polyp segmentation delineates the visible extent of a polyp in a colonoscopy image. The output is a mask that assigns each pixel to foreground or background. A location cue such as a point or a box can narrow the region of interest, but the model must still infer the shape and boundary of the lesion from the image. Variation in apparent size, indistinct transitions between polyps and surrounding tissue, and differences across imaging sources can make this task difficult [1, 2, 3]. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) accepts spatial prompts and predicts a corresponding mask [4]. Its use on colonoscopy images raises two practical questions. The first is how well existing checkpoints segment polyps when given an informative prompt. The second is how much the observed performance changes when the models are adapted to labelled polyp images. MedSAM provides a medical-domain reference [5], while low-rank updates and adapter modules offer different ways to fit a segmentation model to a specific task. These approaches motivate a comparison that gives the evaluated configurations the same prompt information and uses the same outcome definitions. 1.2 Research problem Performance figures from separate publications can describe different tasks. A supervised image-only polyp segmenter receives the image, while a promptable model may also receive a box around the lesion. Training partitions, test datasets and metric implementations can differ as well. To assess the models under a defined condition, this study compares SAM-based configurations on shared evaluation images using boxes generated from the reference masks. The main experiments distinguish zero-shot evaluation from adaptation on the Kvasir-SEG training split [6]. Checkpoints are evaluated on the Kvasir-SEG validation split and CVC-ClinicDB [7]. This design makes it possible to examine performance within the development dataset and on a second dataset. Additional experiments test whether results depend on the prompt setting or vary across images grouped by relative polyp area or boundary contrast. 1.3 Research questions RQ1. Under the same box-prompt protocol, how do zero-shot SAM and MedSAM differ in polyp segmentation performance on the Kvasir-SEG validation split and CVC-ClinicDB? RQ2. After adaptation on the full Kvasir-SEG training split, how do the LoRA and Adapter configurations perform relative to the zero-shot baselines on Kvasir-SEG validation and CVC- ClinicDB? RQ3. For the evaluated models, how does segmentation performance change with prompt type, box expansion or box translation? What differences are observed across groups defined by relative polyp area and boundary contrast? RQ1 and RQ2 define the main comparison. RQ3 is exploratory. The effect of using smaller labelled support sets is examined as a supplementary analysis of RQ2. 1.4 Contributions and scope This thesis establishes a common evaluation protocol for zero-shot and polyp-adapted configurations. It records how the prompts, data partitions and metrics are constructed so that each reported result can be interpreted under its evaluation conditions. It compares LoRA and Adapter configurations trained on Kvasir-SEG against the available zero-shot baselines. PP-SAM provides an existing fine-tuning reference. The comparison reports performance separately for the development and cross-dataset evaluation sets and includes a two-model comparison on two further datasets. It also examines sensitivity to box size, box position and point prompts, and reports descriptive differences across image-area and boundary-contrast groups. The main experiments concern individual images with prompts derived from the annotations. Relative foreground area and contrast describe image properties, and the conclusions apply to the evaluated configurations and prompt conditions. 1.5 Thesis organisation Chapter 2 reviews polyp segmentation, promptable models and adaptation methods. Chapter 3 specifies the datasets, experimental comparisons, prompting protocols and evaluation measures. Chapter 4 describes the implementation and the information required to reproduce the reported results. Chapter 5 presents and discusses the findings for each research question. Chapter 6 summarises the conclusions and outlines further work. 2 2 Related Work 2.1 Endoscopic polyp segmentation Supervised polyp segmentation methods learn to map endoscopic images to binary masks. PraNet combines features into an initial polyp prediction and uses reverse attention to refine regions that the initial prediction leaves unresolved [1]. UACANet explicitly models uncertain regions in an intermediate saliency map and uses this information when refining its predictions [2]. These studies illustrate two reasons to examine more than average overlap. Fine structures may be lost when features are combined across scales, and a plausible foreground region may still have an inaccurate contour. Cross-dataset performance is also relevant. The PolypGen data descriptor documents images and sequences from multiple centres and frames generalisability as a distinct evaluation problem [3]. In this thesis, Kvasir-SEG is the development dataset and CVC-ClinicDB provides the principal cross- dataset comparison [6, 7]. Relative foreground area captures the amount of annotated polyp visible within the image. Boundary contrast provides a separate image-derived description of the transition around the reference contour. Both properties can vary between datasets, which motivates reporting subgroup results separately for each dataset. Image-only methods such as PraNet and UACANet provide context for the task. Their published scores are tied to their training partitions, test sets and input conditions. Here, the central comparison concerns configurations receiving the same annotation-derived box on the same evaluation images. 2.2 SAM and medical-domain adaptation SAM separates image encoding, prompt encoding and mask decoding [4]. The image encoder produces a representation of the frame. The prompt encoder represents the spatial cue. The mask decoder uses both representations to predict the segmentation. A point conveys a location, while a box conveys location and approximate extent. This distinction matters in colonoscopy because the amount of information supplied by the prompt can affect the apparent difficulty of the task. MedSAM adapts the SAM framework using medical images and annotations [5]. It offers a relevant comparison with general-purpose SAM because the two checkpoints reflect different training domains. The comparison in this thesis evaluates both without further fitting on Kvasir-SEG. It therefore addresses the behaviour of the selected pretrained configurations on the evaluated polyp datasets. The same prompting protocol makes their outputs comparable for this task, while checkpoint and input pipeline differences remain part of the configurations being tested. The rank determines the size of that update. SAMed applies this approach to SAM for medical image segmentation [9]. A LoRA configuration can still update components outside the layers with low-rank updates. For reproducibility, the exact set of trainable modules must therefore be stated alongside the rank. Medical SAM Adapter introduces trainable adapter components into SAM for medical image segmentation [10]. A weight update and an inserted module are different mechanisms for adapting a pretrained model. This thesis uses both approaches to evaluate polyp-specific adaptation, including LoRA configurations based on SAMed and MedSAM and ViT-B and ViT-L configurations based on Medical SAM Adapter. The model comparisons reflect the complete recipes, so differences in performance can involve initial weights, architecture, training and inference settings. PP- SAM used perturbed bounding-box prompts during fine-tuning and evaluated sensitivity to box perturbation during inference [11]. It is relevant to this study because it links two distinct experimental choices. The number of labelled training images determines the supervision available during adaptation. The prompt supplied at inference determines the spatial information available for a particular prediction. These choices are evaluated separately here. The annotation-budget comparison reports SAMed and PP-SAM runs trained with 1, 5, 10 and 880 images. The prompt comparisons reuse fixed checkpoints and change the prompts at inference. Outward expansion changes the amount of surrounding tissue included in the box. Translation changes the position of a box whose dimensions are held fixed. A point and a box supply different spatial cues, so the point-versus-box comparison concerns a change in prompt representation. 2.5 Position of this study Prior work provides supervised systems for delineating polyps, general and medical promptable checkpoints, and several ways to adapt the checkpoints with labelled data. The present study brings selected configurations together under a common box-prompt protocol for two-dimensional colonoscopy images. It compares zero-shot and polyp-adapted performance across the designated datasets and then examines where outcomes differ with prompt conditions, foreground area and boundary contrast. These complementary comparisons connect the methods reviewed in this chapter to the experimental design in Chapter 3. 5 3 Research Methodology 3.1 Study design This study evaluates promptable segmentation models on two-dimensional colonoscopy images. Let the box coordinates be x min , y min , x max and y max , with maxima defined by the foreground pixel indices. Each minimum is decreased by the chosen expansion and each maximum is increased by the same amount. The resulting coordinates are clipped to the image bounds using the evaluation code's coordinate convention. The default expansion is 30 pixels. Prompt generation takes place before any model-specific coordinate transformation. The same prompt is supplied to every configuration in the main comparison. The expansion experiment uses deterministic outward expansions of 0, 10, 30 and 50 pixels on selected trained configurations. The local SAMed expansion series uses its own 30-pixel rerun, while the main comparison retains the formal SAMed checkpoint evaluation. For translations, the 30-pixel reference box is shifted in each cardinal direction. The requested displacement is 5% of 8 the corresponding image dimension, rounded to a whole pixel. When the image edge prevents the full shift, the displacement is reduced while box width and height are preserved. The overall analysis retains these cases. A supplementary comparison records results for the subset that received the full requested displacement in each direction. Point prompts are derived from the same annotation. One condition uses the foreground pixel farthest from the background, with ties resolved in row-major order. A second condition adds one sampled negative point from the background using base seed 24. A third condition samples one positive point uniformly from the foreground for each of three base seeds (24, 42 and 1234). The seed for a frame is derived from the base seed and a stable hash of the image identifier. Within each model, its decoder and mask-selection settings are held constant across prompt conditions. 3.5 Evaluation and subgroup analysis 3.5.1 Evaluation metrics Metrics are computed between a binary prediction and the reference mask at native image resolution. Let TP, FP and FN denote the numbers of true-positive, false-positive and false-negative foreground pixels. The Dice coefficient is 2TP/(2TP + FP + FN), and intersection over union (IoU) is TP/(TP + FP + FN). Precision is TP/(TP + FP), and recall is TP/(TP + FN). Reported precision follows the archived implementation's convention of assigning 1 when the prediction contains no positive pixels. For an empty prediction on an image with annotated foreground, Dice, IoU, recall and boundary F1 are zero. The reported segmentation means give one weight to each evaluated image. Standard deviations, where reported, describe variation across images. Boundary F1 measures contour agreement. A boundary pixel is obtained from the difference between a foreground mask and its four-connected erosion. The matching tolerance is 0.5% of the image diagonal, rounded to a whole pixel with a minimum of one pixel. Boundary precision is the proportion of predicted boundary pixels within the tolerance of the reference boundary. Boundary recall uses the reverse direction. Their harmonic mean gives boundary F1. Surface HD95 describes contour distance. For each boundary pixel, find the distance to its nearest point on the other boundary. Take the 95th percentile separately in each direction and report the larger value in original-image pixels. Surface HD95 is undefined for an empty prediction and the table gives the number of finite measurements. The same implementation was applied to saved predictions in all 28 rows of the main results table. The saved predictions were reconciled against the recorded overlap metrics before recomputation. 3.5.2 Image subgroup definitions Relative polyp area is the fraction of original-image pixels labelled as foreground. The primary groups use area fractions of at most 5%, greater than 5% up to 20%, and greater than 20%. A sensitivity analysis also uses the area tertiles estimated from the 880 Kvasir training masks and applies those 9 cutoffs to both primary evaluation sets. Each area group describes how much of the image is occupied by annotated foreground. Boundary contrast is the Euclidean distance between the mean CIELAB colours in narrow bands immediately inside and outside the annotated boundary. The primary band width is 0.5% of the image diagonal, rounded to the nearest pixel with a minimum of two pixels. The lower and upper quartiles within each dataset define the low, intermediate and high contrast groups. Alternative band widths and the median grayscale gradient magnitude along the annotated boundary provide sensitivity checks. Area and contrast groups are also crossed to examine how their overlap may affect the descriptive results. Thirty images from each primary dataset were sampled without replacement using a fixed seed. After five warm-up images per dataset and configuration, the same 30 images were used in three timed passes. GPU work was synchronised around each timer. Latency includes model-specific preprocessing, image encoding, prompt decoding and returning the mask to the host. The timed interval excludes disk reads, model loading and prompt generation. Each model retained 10 its configured input resolution. Frames per second are computed by dividing 1,000 by the mean latency in milliseconds. 3.6 Statistical analysis and validity 3.6.1 Statistical comparisons For the point and box-translation study, the effect for a frame is the metric under the tested prompt minus the metric under that frame's 30-pixel reference box. The three trials for the random-positive condition are averaged within each frame before aggregation. This yields a matched comparison of prompts for each configuration. For CVC-ClinicDB, frame-level Dice changes are averaged within each of the 29 video sequences, then averaged equally across sequences. The exploratory analysis considers 21 contrasts across seven nonreference prompt conditions. These comprise the paired change within SAM ViT-B, the paired change within MSA-B and the difference between the two configurations' paired changes. Two-sided Monte Carlo sign-flip tests use 100,000 draws and assume independent, symmetric sequence-level paired differences under the null. Holm correction is applied across all 21 contrasts. Unadjusted 95% percentile intervals are obtained by bootstrapping sequences. Kvasir prompt results and the image subgroup comparisons are reported descriptively. 3.6.2 Validity of comparisons The annotation-derived box and points provide lesion location information during evaluation. The resulting comparisons characterise segmentation under supplied prompts. The validation scores for Kvasir-SEG also reflect checkpoint selection, and the local CVC sequence groups define the sampling unit for the statistical analysis. The area and contrast analyses were conducted after the primary experiments. A fixed expansion in pixels changes the box size proportionally more for smaller boxes, so differences between area groups may reflect a combination of foreground scale and prompt geometry. The colour and gradient measurements are image-derived proxies for boundary visibility and can be affected by illumination, texture and reflections. Expert boundary ratings would be needed to determine how closely these proxies agree with human assessments. The few-shot pilot addresses training variation only for its own short schedule and the tested support draws. 11 4 Design and Implementation 4.1 Experimental pipeline Figure 4.1 shows how the project prepared the zero-shot and adapted checkpoints and evaluated them on the two primary datasets. The zero-shot branch loaded the released SAM ViT-B, SAM ViT-L and MedSAM ViT-B weights. The adaptation branch fitted the specified modules using images from the Kvasir-SEG training list and selected checkpoints using mean Dice on the Kvasir-SEG validation list. Each selected configuration was then evaluated on Kvasir-SEG validation and CVC-ClinicDB. The evaluation scripts applied the common box rule in the original image coordinates, then passed the image and prompt through each model's input transformation and decoder. Figure 4.1 Training and evaluation pipeline for the primary comparison. The adapted branch uses Kvasir-SEG validation Dice for checkpoint selection. The evaluation branch constructs the reference box from the corresponding frame annotation. 4.2 Input preprocessing and prompt preparation Evaluation scripts paired each frame with its annotation by image identifier and decoded the frame as RGB. They converted the grayscale annotation into a binary reference, treating values above 127 as foreground. The scripts constructed prompts on that original image grid and then mapped the prompt coordinates together with the image into the model's input space. SAM, MedSAM, both LoRA configurations and PP-SAM used the SAM predictor. The predictor resized the longest image side to 1,024 pixels while preserving aspect ratio, then normalised and padded the image for the encoder. It transformed the prompt coordinates using the resized image 12 dimensions. MSA-B and MSA-L used a 512 × 512 square resize with bilinear interpolation, followed by the adapter model's preprocessing. Prompt coordinates were scaled separately along the horizontal and vertical axes, using the ratio of 512 to the original width and height respectively. Thus, models could receive the same prompt on the original frame while operating on different input grids. 4.3 Model adaptation and training The adaptation runs differed in their starting weights, trainable modules and training prompts. The SAMed and MedSAM + LoRA scripts attached rank-four low-rank updates to the query and value projections in the image encoder. The pretrained image encoder weights remained frozen, and the prompt encoder and mask decoder also received gradient updates. Each run updated approximately 4.21 million parameters, or 4.49% of the instantiated model. Both scripts used tight annotation boxes during training and combined binary cross-entropy and Dice losses with weights of 0.2 and 0.8. The full-data runs used a 250-iteration learning-rate warm-up, polynomial decay, and AdamW weight decay of 0.1. The two MSA runs updated the adapter layers within the image encoder and the mask decoder. The training loop computed prompt embeddings without gradients. It sampled a positive point from the 13 target during training and used a foreground-weighted binary cross-entropy loss at a mask output size of 256 × 256. The default evaluation used the expanded box defined in Section 3.4. PP-SAM updated the full image encoder and prompt encoder with a frozen mask decoder. For training, it expanded each side of the tight box by an independently sampled integer between 0 and 50 pixels and clipped the coordinates to the image bounds. The training objective combined spatially weighted binary cross-entropy and IoU losses. This randomised training prompt preceded the deterministic box construction used at evaluation. The annotation-budget experiments used separate SAMed and PP-SAM runs at 1, 5 and 10 labelled images. The archived SAMed few-shot runs used 100 epochs and a peak learning rate of 0.005. PP- SAM used its 100-epoch schedule at a learning rate of 0.0001 across budgets. The SAMed budget comparison therefore reflects the recorded recipes at each budget, including their different learning rates and schedules. 4.4 Inference and evaluation outputs The SAM predictor configurations requested one candidate mask per prompt. The predictor restored the selected logits to native resolution, removed the input padding during that transformation and thresholded the restored logits at zero. The MSA evaluators requested multiple candidates and selected the mask with the highest model-predicted IoU score. They bilinearly resized that mask's logits to 512 × 512, applied a sigmoid and binarised at a probability threshold of 0.5. They then used nearest-neighbour interpolation to restore the binary mask to the original frame dimensions. Metric computation in both paths used the restored binary mask. During the dedicated point and box- translation experiments, each model reused the image embedding for the alternative prompts on the same frame. The evaluators wrote a predicted mask and per-image metric record keyed by image identifier, and a JSON summary for each run. The thesis results use those records for overlap metrics. For surface HD95, a common post-evaluation script paired the saved predictions with reference masks, verified the pairings by reconciling Dice and IoU with the recorded scores, and recomputed the contour statistic defined in Section 3.5.1. The aggregation retained the number of images with a defined surface distance. 4.5 Reproducibility records To reproduce a row of the main table, start with its model configuration and selected checkpoint, the evaluation image list and the prompt specification.
