1 Introduction

1.1 Background and motivation

Endoscopic polyp segmentation delineates the visible extent of a polyp in a colonoscopy image. The
output is a mask that assigns each pixel to foreground or background. A location cue such as a point
or a box can narrow the region of interest, but the model must still infer the shape and boundary of
the lesion from the image. Variation in apparent size, indistinct transitions between polyps and
surrounding tissue, and differences across imaging sources can make this task difficult [1, 2, 3].

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) accepts spatial prompts and predicts a corresponding mask [4].
Its use on colonoscopy images raises two practical questions. The first is how well existing
checkpoints segment polyps when given an informative prompt. The second is how much the
observed performance changes when the models are adapted to labelled polyp images. MedSAM
provides a medical-domain reference [5], while low-rank updates and adapter modules offer different
ways to fit a segmentation model to a specific task. These approaches motivate a comparison that
gives the evaluated configurations the same prompt information and uses the same outcome
definitions.

1.2 Research problem

Performance figures from separate publications can describe different tasks. A supervised image-only
polyp segmenter receives the image, while a promptable model may also receive a box around the
lesion. Training partitions, test datasets and metric implementations can differ as well. To assess the
models under a defined condition, this study compares SAM-based configurations on shared
evaluation images using boxes generated from the reference masks.
The main experiments distinguish zero-shot evaluation from adaptation on the Kvasir-SEG training
split [6]. Checkpoints are evaluated on the Kvasir-SEG validation split and CVC-ClinicDB [7]. This
design makes it possible to examine performance within the development dataset and on a second
dataset. Additional experiments test whether results depend on the prompt setting or vary across
images grouped by relative polyp area or boundary contrast.
1.3 Research questions

RQ1. Under the same box-prompt protocol, how do zero-shot SAM and MedSAM differ in polyp
segmentation performance on the Kvasir-SEG validation split and CVC-ClinicDB?
RQ2. After adaptation on the full Kvasir-SEG training split, how do the LoRA and Adapter
configurations perform relative to the zero-shot baselines on Kvasir-SEG validation and CVC-
ClinicDB?

RQ3. For the evaluated models, how does segmentation performance change with prompt type, box
expansion or box translation? What differences are observed across groups defined by relative polyp
area and boundary contrast?
RQ1 and RQ2 define the main comparison. RQ3 is exploratory. The effect of using smaller labelled
support sets is examined as a supplementary analysis of RQ2.

1.4 Contributions and scope

This thesis establishes a common evaluation protocol for zero-shot and polyp-adapted configurations.
It records how the prompts, data partitions and metrics are constructed so that each reported result
can be interpreted under its evaluation conditions.
It compares LoRA and Adapter configurations trained on Kvasir-SEG against the available zero-shot
baselines. PP-SAM provides an existing fine-tuning reference. The primary comparison reports
within-dataset and cross-dataset performance. A follow-up evaluates five fixed configurations on two
additional datasets.
It also examines sensitivity to box size, box position and point prompts, and reports descriptive
differences across image-area and boundary-contrast groups. The main experiments concern
individual images with prompts derived from the annotations. Relative foreground area and contrast
describe image properties, and the conclusions apply to the evaluated configurations and prompt
conditions.

1.5 Thesis organisation

Chapter 2 reviews polyp segmentation, promptable models and adaptation methods. Chapter 3
specifies the datasets, experimental comparisons, prompting protocols and evaluation measures.
Chapter 4 describes the implementation and the information required to reproduce the reported results.
Chapter 5 presents and discusses the findings for each research question. Chapter 6 summarises the
conclusions and outlines further work.

2 Related Work

2.1 Endoscopic polyp segmentation

Supervised polyp segmentation methods learn to map endoscopic images to binary masks. PraNet
combines features into an initial polyp prediction and uses reverse attention to refine regions that the
initial prediction leaves unresolved [1]. UACANet explicitly models uncertain regions in an
intermediate saliency map and uses this information when refining its predictions [2]. These studies
illustrate two reasons to examine more than average overlap. Fine structures may be lost when
features are combined across scales, and a plausible foreground region may still have an inaccurate
contour.
Cross-dataset performance is also relevant. The PolypGen data descriptor documents images and
sequences from multiple centres and frames generalisability as a distinct evaluation problem [3]. In
this thesis, Kvasir-SEG is the development dataset and CVC-ClinicDB provides the principal cross-
dataset comparison [6, 7]. Relative foreground area captures the amount of annotated polyp visible
within the image. Boundary contrast provides a separate image-derived description of the transition
around the reference contour. Both properties can vary between datasets, which motivates reporting
subgroup results separately for each dataset.

Image-only methods such as PraNet and UACANet provide context for the task. Their published
scores are tied to their training partitions, test sets and input conditions. Here, the central comparison
concerns configurations receiving the same annotation-derived box on the same evaluation images.

2.2 SAM and medical-domain adaptation

SAM separates image encoding, prompt encoding and mask decoding [4]. The image encoder
produces a representation of the frame. The prompt encoder represents the spatial cue. The mask
decoder uses both representations to predict the segmentation. A point conveys a location, while a
box conveys location and approximate extent. This distinction matters in colonoscopy because the
amount of information supplied by the prompt can affect the apparent difficulty of the task.
MedSAM adapts the SAM framework using medical images and annotations [5]. It offers a relevant
comparison with general-purpose SAM because the two checkpoints reflect different training
domains. The comparison in this thesis evaluates both without further fitting on Kvasir-SEG. It
therefore addresses the behaviour of the selected pretrained configurations on the evaluated polyp
datasets. The same prompting protocol makes their outputs comparable for this task, while checkpoint
and input pipeline differences remain part of the configurations being tested.

2.3 Parameter-efficient adaptation

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) represents a weight update as the product of two smaller trainable
matrices [8]. The rank determines the size of that update. SAMed applies this approach to SAM for
medical image segmentation [9]. A LoRA configuration can still update components outside the
layers with low-rank updates. For reproducibility, the exact set of trainable modules must therefore
be stated alongside the rank.

Medical SAM Adapter introduces trainable adapter components into SAM for medical image
segmentation [10]. A weight update and an inserted module are different mechanisms for adapting a
pretrained model. This thesis uses both approaches to evaluate polyp-specific adaptation, including
LoRA configurations based on SAMed and MedSAM and ViT-B and ViT-L configurations based on
Medical SAM Adapter. The model comparisons reflect the complete recipes, so differences in
performance can involve initial weights, architecture, training and inference settings.

2.4 Limited annotation and prompt sensitivity

Few-shot adaptation examines what can be achieved with a small set of labelled training images. PP-
SAM used perturbed bounding-box prompts during fine-tuning and evaluated sensitivity to box
perturbation during inference [11]. It is relevant to this study because it links two distinct
experimental choices. The number of labelled training images determines the supervision available
during adaptation. The prompt supplied at inference determines the spatial information available for
a particular prediction.
These choices are evaluated separately here. The annotation-budget comparison reports SAMed and
PP-SAM runs trained with 1, 5, 10 and 880 images. The prompt comparisons reuse fixed checkpoints
and change the prompts at inference. Outward expansion changes the amount of surrounding tissue
included in the box. Translation changes the position of a box whose dimensions are held fixed. A
point and a box supply different spatial cues, so the point-versus-box comparison concerns a change
in prompt representation.

2.5 Position of this study

Prior work provides supervised systems for delineating polyps, general and medical promptable
checkpoints, and several ways to adapt the checkpoints with labelled data. The present study brings
selected configurations together under a common box-prompt protocol for two-dimensional
colonoscopy images. It compares zero-shot and polyp-adapted performance across the designated
datasets and then examines where outcomes differ with prompt conditions, foreground area and
boundary contrast. These complementary comparisons connect the methods reviewed in this chapter
to the experimental design in Chapter 3.

3.4 Prompt construction and variants

The default evaluation prompt was derived from the tight bounding box of the annotated foreground
in the original image. Let the box coordinates be xmin, ymin, xmax and ymax, with maxima defined by the
foreground pixel indices. Each minimum is decreased by the chosen expansion and each maximum
is increased by the same amount. The resulting coordinates are clipped to the image bounds using the
evaluation code's coordinate convention. The default expansion is 30 pixels. Prompt generation takes
place before any model-specific coordinate transformation. The same prompt is supplied to every
configuration in the main comparison.

The point and box-translation experiments use zero-shot SAM ViT-B and MSA-B. For translations,
the 30-pixel reference box is shifted in each cardinal direction. The requested displacement is 5% of
the corresponding image dimension, rounded to a whole pixel. When the image edge prevents the full

retains these cases. A supplementary comparison records results for the subset that received the full

Point prompts are derived from the same annotation. One condition uses the foreground pixel farthest
from the background, with ties resolved in row-major order. A second condition adds one sampled
negative point from the background using base seed 24. A third condition samples one positive point
uniformly from the foreground for each of three base seeds (24, 42 and 1234). The seed for a frame
is derived from the base seed and a stable hash of the image identifier. Within each model, its decoder
and mask-selection settings are held constant across prompt conditions.

3.5 Evaluation and subgroup analysis

3.5.1 Evaluation metrics
Metrics are computed between a binary prediction and the reference mask at native image resolution.
Let TP, FP and FN denote the numbers of true-positive, false-positive and false-negative foreground
pixels. The Dice coefficient is 2TP/(2TP + FP + FN), and intersection over union (IoU) is TP/(TP +
FP + FN). Precision is TP/(TP + FP), and recall is TP/(TP + FN). Reported precision follows the
archived implementation's convention of assigning 1 when the prediction contains no positive pixels.
For an empty prediction on an image with annotated foreground, Dice, IoU, recall and boundary F1
are zero. The reported segmentation means give one weight to each evaluated image. Standard
deviations, where reported, describe variation across images.
Boundary F1 measures contour agreement. A boundary pixel is obtained from the difference between
a foreground mask and its four-connected erosion. The matching tolerance is 0.5% of the image
diagonal, rounded to a whole pixel with a minimum of one pixel. Boundary precision is the proportion
of predicted boundary pixels within the tolerance of the reference boundary. Boundary recall uses the
reverse direction. Their harmonic mean gives boundary F1.
Surface HD95 describes contour distance. For each boundary pixel, find the distance to its nearest
point on the other boundary. Take the 95th percentile separately in each direction and report the larger
value in original-image pixels. Surface HD95 is undefined for an empty prediction and the table gives
the number of finite measurements. The same implementation was applied to saved predictions in all
28 rows of the main results table. The saved predictions were reconciled against the recorded overlap
metrics before recomputation.

3.5.2 Image subgroup definitions
Relative polyp area is the fraction of original-image pixels labelled as foreground. The primary groups
use area fractions of at most 5%, greater than 5% up to 20%, and greater than 20%. A sensitivity
analysis also uses the area tertiles estimated from the 880 Kvasir training masks and applies those
cutoffs to both primary evaluation sets. Each area group describes how much of the image is occupied
by annotated foreground.
Boundary contrast is the Euclidean distance between the mean CIELAB colours in narrow bands
immediately inside and outside the annotated boundary. The primary band width is 0.5% of the image
diagonal, rounded to the nearest pixel with a minimum of two pixels. The lower and upper quartiles
within each dataset define the low, intermediate and high contrast groups. Alternative band widths
and the median grayscale gradient magnitude along the annotated boundary provide sensitivity checks.
Area and contrast groups are also crossed to examine how their overlap may affect the descriptive
results.

3.5.3 Inference timing
SAM ViT-B, SAM ViT-L and MSA-B were benchmarked at batch size one on an NVIDIA GeForce
RTX 4070 Ti SUPER. Thirty images from each primary dataset were sampled without replacement
using a fixed seed. After five warm-up images per dataset and configuration, the same 30 images
                                                                                                                  10
were used in three timed passes. GPU work was synchronised around each timer. Latency includes
model-specific preprocessing, image encoding, prompt decoding and returning the mask to the host.
The timed interval excludes disk reads, model loading and prompt generation. Each model retained
its configured input resolution. Frames per second are computed by dividing 1,000 by the mean
latency in milliseconds.

3.6 Statistical analysis and validity

3.6.1 Statistical comparisons
For the point and box-translation study, the effect for a frame is the metric under the tested prompt
minus the metric under that frame's 30-pixel reference box. The three trials for the random-positive
condition are averaged within each frame before aggregation. This yields a matched comparison of
prompts for each configuration.
For CVC-ClinicDB, frame-level Dice changes are averaged within each of the 29 video sequences,
then averaged equally across sequences. The exploratory analysis considers 21 contrasts across seven
nonreference prompt conditions. These comprise the paired change within SAM ViT-B, the paired
change within MSA-B and the difference between the two configurations' paired changes. Two-sided
Monte Carlo sign-flip tests use 100,000 draws and assume independent, symmetric sequence-level
paired differences under the null. Holm correction is applied across all 21 contrasts. Unadjusted 95%
percentile intervals are obtained by bootstrapping sequences. Kvasir prompt results and the image
subgroup comparisons are reported descriptively.

3.6.2 Validity of comparisons
The annotation-derived box and points provide lesion location information during evaluation. The
resulting comparisons characterise segmentation under supplied prompts. The validation scores for
Kvasir-SEG also reflect checkpoint selection, and the local CVC sequence groups define the sampling
unit for the statistical analysis.

The area and contrast analyses were conducted after the primary experiments. A fixed expansion in
pixels changes the box size proportionally more for smaller boxes, so differences between area groups
may reflect a combination of foreground scale and prompt geometry. The colour and gradient
measurements are image-derived proxies for boundary visibility and can be affected by illumination,
texture and reflections. Expert boundary ratings would be needed to determine how closely these
proxies agree with human assessments. The few-shot pilot addresses training variation only for its
own short schedule and the tested support draws.

4 Design and Implementation

4.1 Experimental pipeline

The pipeline loaded released weights for the zero-shot baselines and fitted the adapted configurations
on the Kvasir-SEG training partition. Checkpoint selection used mean Dice on Kvasir-SEG validation.
Both branches converged at evaluation, where the scripts passed a common image and annotation-
derived box through each configuration's input transformation and decoder (Figure 4.1).




Figure 4.1 Training and evaluation pipeline for the primary comparison. Reference boxes for inference are derived from
the corresponding frame annotations.


4.2 Input preprocessing and prompt preparation

Evaluation scripts paired each frame with its annotation by image identifier and decoded the frame
as RGB. They converted the grayscale annotation into a binary reference, treating values above 127
as foreground. The scripts constructed prompts on that original image grid and then mapped the
prompt coordinates together with the image into the model's input space.
SAM, MedSAM, both LoRA configurations and PP-SAM used the SAM predictor. It resized the
longest side to 1,024 pixels while preserving aspect ratio, then normalised and padded the image for
the encoder. It scaled the prompt coordinates using the resized dimensions. MSA-B and MSA-L used
bilinear interpolation to resize images to 512 × 512 before model preprocessing. Horizontal and
vertical prompt coordinates were scaled by 512 divided by the original width and height, respectively.

The SAMed and MedSAM + LoRA scripts attached rank-four low-rank updates to the query and
value projections in the image encoder. The pretrained image encoder weights remained frozen, and
the prompt encoder and mask decoder also received gradient updates. Each run updated
approximately 4.21 million parameters, or 4.49% of the instantiated model. Both scripts used tight
annotation boxes during training and combined binary cross-entropy and Dice losses with weights of
0.2 and 0.8. The full-data runs used a 250-iteration learning-rate warm-up, polynomial decay, and
AdamW weight decay of 0.1.
For MSA-B and MSA-L, the training loop encoded a sampled positive point without updating the
prompt encoder. It used foreground-weighted binary cross-entropy at a mask output size of 256 ×
256. The default evaluation used the expanded box defined in Section 3.4.

PP-SAM updated the full image encoder and prompt encoder with a frozen mask decoder. For training,
it expanded each side of the tight box by an independently sampled integer between 0 and 50 pixels
and clipped the coordinates to the image bounds. The training objective combined spatially weighted
binary cross-entropy and IoU losses. This randomised training prompt preceded the deterministic box
construction used at evaluation.

4.4 Inference and evaluation outputs

The SAM predictor configurations requested one candidate mask per prompt. The predictor restored
the selected logits to native resolution, removed the input padding during that transformation and
thresholded the restored logits at zero. The MSA evaluators requested multiple candidates and
selected the mask with the highest model-predicted IoU score. They bilinearly resized that mask's
logits to 512 ×512, applied a sigmoid and binarised at a probability threshold of 0.5. They then used
nearest-neighbour interpolation to restore the binary mask to the original frame dimensions. Metric
computation in both paths used the restored binary mask. During the dedicated point and box-
translation experiments, each model reused the image embedding for the alternative prompts on the
same frame.

The evaluators wrote a predicted mask and per-image metric record keyed by image identifier, and a
JSON summary for each run. The thesis results use those records for overlap metrics. For surface
HD95, a common post-evaluation script paired the saved predictions with reference masks, verified
the pairings by reconciling Dice and IoU with the recorded scores, and recomputed the contour
statistic defined in Section 3.5.1. The aggregation retained the number of images with a defined
surface distance.

4.5 Reproducibility records

To reproduce a row of the main table, start with its model configuration and selected checkpoint, the
evaluation image list and the prompt specification. The run index links configurations to the archived
summaries and per-image outputs. The training logs record the initial weights, trainable modules,
schedule and checkpoint-selection results. For a few-shot run, the support sample and sampling seed

SAM-L had the highest mean Dice and IoU among the zero-shot configurations on both datasets. Its
Dice was 0.8140 on Kvasir validation and 0.6588 on CVC-ClinicDB, compared with 0.7497 and
0.5182 for SAM-B. All three zero-shot configurations had lower mean Dice on CVC-ClinicDB than
on Kvasir validation. The size of the difference varied by checkpoint.
MedSAM reached a mean Dice of 0.7034 on Kvasir and 0.5172 on CVC. On CVC its Dice was close
to SAM-B's, but its higher recall (0.7672 versus 0.6377) came with lower precision (0.4096 versus
0.4767) and substantially lower boundary F1 (0.0863 versus 0.2950). Under the shared box protocol,
medical pretraining alone did not confer a zero-shot Dice advantage for this MedSAM checkpoint.

5.2 Polyp-specific adaptation (RQ2)

5.2.1 Full-data comparison
Each full-data LoRA or Adapter configuration improved mean Dice over its corresponding zero-shot
baseline on both primary datasets. SAMed improved over SAM-B by 0.1457 on Kvasir and 0.3427
on CVC. MedSAM+LoRA improved over MedSAM by 0.2014 and 0.3304, respectively. For the
adapters, MSA-B reached 0.8524 on Kvasir and 0.7685 on CVC, while MSA-L reached 0.8555 and
0.8234. The adapter comparisons reflect the full configurations, including the input resolutions and
mask-selection policies described in Chapter 4.
PP-SAM had the highest mean Dice on Kvasir (0.9226) and the lowest mean surface HD95 on both
datasets. On CVC, SAMed and PP-SAM were close in mean Dice (0.8608 and 0.8576). SAMed had
higher boundary F1 on both sets, whereas PP-SAM had lower mean surface HD95. The measures
capture different aspects of the contour: boundary F1 counts contour agreement within a tolerance,
while surface HD95 summarises distances near the tail of the contour-distance distribution. The small
CVC Dice difference is descriptive and does not establish a stable ranking across runs.

5.3.1 Box expansion
Figure 5.1 shows mean Dice at fixed outward expansions of 0, 10, 30 and 50 pixels. In every plotted

For MSA-B on Kvasir, Dice was 0.8573 at both 0 and 10 pixels to four decimal places and 0.8462 at

5.6 Interpretation and limitations

The full-data adapted configurations improved on their corresponding baselines in the primary
comparison. The follow-up extended this comparison to five configurations and found that their
ordering depended on the dataset. All four adapted configurations exceeded SAM-B on CVC-
ColonDB, and SAM-B led on ETIS-LaribPolypDB. The few-shot comparisons and short repeatability
pilot indicate sensitivity to the training recipe and support images. Kvasir validation also informed
checkpoint selection, so CVC-ClinicDB provides the main comparison on data excluded from
adaptation and selection. The CVC sequence-level tests concern prompt changes on fixed checkpoints.

The reference prompts supplied lesion location from the annotations. The resulting scores characterise
segmentation given that information. In the exploratory automatic-prompt pilot, the teacher-generated
box followed by SAM-B yielded Dice of 0.2644 on Kvasir and 0.1298 on CVC, with missing boxes
counted as empty outputs. This comparison illustrates the dependence on prompt generation for that
particular pipeline. Claims about boundary visibility are limited to the image-derived proxy, and the
speed estimates apply to the tested hardware, resolutions and inference paths.

6 Conclusion and Future Work

6.1 Answers to the research questions

For RQ1, SAM-L led the zero-shot configurations in mean Dice on Kvasir-SEG validation (0.8140)
and CVC-ClinicDB (0.6588). On CVC-ClinicDB, SAM-B and MedSAM had similar Dice. MedSAM
recorded higher recall, while SAM-B achieved higher precision and boundary F1.
For RQ2, the four full-data LoRA and Adapter configurations improved mean Dice over their paired
zero-shot baselines on both primary datasets. PP-SAM led on Kvasir-SEG validation (0.9226) and
SAMed on CVC-ClinicDB (0.8608). In the external follow-up, all four adapted configurations
exceeded SAM-B on CVC-ColonDB, where PP-SAM led at 0.8493. On ETIS-LaribPolypDB, SAM-
B led at 0.8613, followed by PP-SAM at 0.8436.

For RQ3, 50-pixel expansion reduced mean Dice relative to 0 and 10 pixels in every verified series.
Point prompts improved Dice for SAM-B and MSA-B on CVC-ClinicDB. On Kvasir, they reduced
Dice for SAM-B and slightly improved it for MSA-B. Translation effects varied by direction and
model. Area and contrast group rankings varied by model, dataset and metric.

6.2 Contributions and scope

The study compared released and polyp-adapted checkpoints under a common 30-pixel box protocol
and recomputed surface HD95 from saved masks using one definition. The main results describe
single-frame segmentation given annotation-derived location cues. The formal few-shot comparisons
use one training run per method and budget. The boundary analysis uses an image-derived contrast
proxy.

6.3 Future work

Independent endoscopy expert ratings can test whether the contrast proxy reflects perceived boundary
visibility. Report agreement among raters and with the proxy.

Repeat the budget study with multiple support draws and optimisation seeds under matched training
schedules to estimate the effect of label count more reliably.

Evaluate an image-based localiser with fixed segmentation checkpoints on held-out data. Report
missed proposals and mask errors on prompted frames to identify where accuracy is lost.
